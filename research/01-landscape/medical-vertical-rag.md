# Medical & Biomedical RAG and the Lessons of Regulated Deployment (as of mid-2026)

## Scope

Medicine is the largest and oldest application vertical for retrieval-augmented generation, and the one where the gap between benchmark performance and deployable performance has been measured most carefully. This document covers:

- The benchmark line that defined the field: **MIRAGE / MedRAG** and what it actually established about when retrieval helps and when it hurts medical QA.
- Self-reflective and iterative medical RAG: **Self-BioRAG**, **i-MedRAG**, **Self-MedRAG**, rationale-guided and multi-agent variants.
- **Almanac** (NEJM AI) and the small set of studies that evaluated clinical RAG with clinicians rather than with multiple-choice exams.
- Biomedical **retrieval infrastructure**: PubMed/MedCorp corpora, MedCPT, BioLORD-2023, UMLS-grounded retrieval, and the multilingual retrieval cliff.
- **Ontology- and guideline-grounded** medical RAG (MedGraphRAG, ChronoMedKG, guideline-anchored RAG), covered only for what is *medical* about it — the graph mechanics themselves live in `graph-structured-rag.md`.
- **Deployment evidence 2024–2026**: on-premise hospital agentic RAG, EHR-embedded clinical LLM systems, simulation studies with prescribing-error endpoints, and the commercial point-of-care RAG products (OpenEvidence and peers) that clinicians are already using without being told to.
- **Pharma-specific** RAG: pharmacovigilance and adverse-event extraction, drug-information QA with authority hierarchies, cGMP/regulatory compliance agents, systematic-review and literature automation, clinical-trial document assistants.
- **Regulated-deployment requirements**: provenance and citation validity, corpus versioning and temporal validity, abstention/deferral, audit trails, bias, security, and corpus integrity under adversarial pressure.
- The generalizable design requirements a high-stakes vertical imposes on *any* next-generation RAG framework.

Audience: ML-systems researchers designing a next-generation agentic RAG framework. Emphasis is on failure modes, critiques, and open problems. Peer-reviewed clinical literature, arXiv preprints, and vendor/product claims are distinguished throughout.

## Lineage & key work

**2023 — retrieval infrastructure arrives before medical RAG does.**

- **MedCPT** — Jin et al. — arXiv:2307.00589 — 2023. Contrastively pre-trained retriever/re-ranker pair trained on **255 million PubMed user click logs**; first credible zero-shot semantic retriever for biomedicine, reported SOTA on six biomedical IR tasks and beating much larger models including GPT-3-sized `cpt-text-XL`. *Limitations:* click logs encode literature-search intent, not clinical-decision intent; the corpus is English PubMed; no notion of evidence quality or recency in the ranking function. Journal venue not verified in this pass *[uncertain]*.
- **Almanac (preprint)** — Hiesinger, Zakka et al. — Research Square 10.21203/rs.3.rs-2883198/v1 — 2023. First clinical RAG system evaluated by clinicians across specialties; reported "significant increases in factuality (mean of 18% at p-value < 0.05) across all specialties."

**2024 — the benchmark year.**

- **Self-BioRAG** — Jeong et al. — ISMB 2024 (arXiv:2401.15269) — 2024. Biomedical adaptation of Self-RAG: 84,000 filtered biomedical instruction sets, custom reflective tokens that decide *whether* to retrieve and *whether* the generation is supported; **+7.2% absolute average** over the best open foundation model ≤7B on three medical QA benchmarks. *Limitations:* reflective tokens are trained on synthetic supervision; gains are measured on multiple-choice QA, not on long-form or abstention-sensitive tasks; ≤7B comparison class.
- **MIRAGE + MedRAG** — Xiong et al. — arXiv:2402.13178 — Feb 2024. *(Name collision warning: this is **not** MIRAGE-Bench, Thakur et al. arXiv:2410.13716, the multilingual RAG arena covered in `evaluation-benchmarks.md`, and not the several unrelated 2024–26 vision-language papers using the same acronym — arXiv:2605.04772, 2603.21687, 2410.09729. Disambiguate explicitly in any related-work section.)* The field-defining benchmark: **7,663 questions** from five medical QA datasets, **41 combinations** of corpus × retriever × backbone LLM, over **1.8 trillion prompt tokens**. Headline: MedRAG improves accuracy of six LLMs **by up to 18%** over chain-of-thought, "elevating the performance of GPT-3.5 and Mixtral to GPT-4-level." Two structural findings matter more than the headline (see *When RAG helps and hurts*): a **log-linear scaling** curve in retrieved-snippet count for k ≤ 32, and a **"lost-in-the-middle"** U-shape in accuracy as a function of the position of the ground-truth snippet. The project page adds that **only PubMed and the aggregated MedCorp corpus let MedRAG beat CoT on all tasks**, that MedCPT was the most reliable retriever with BM25 a strong lexical alternative, and that retriever fusion "may not always lead to a better performance." Venue not verified in this pass *[uncertain]*.
- **Almanac** — Zakka et al. — **NEJM AI**, DOI 10.1056/aioa2300068 — 2024. LLM augmented with retrieval over *curated* medical resources for guideline and treatment recommendations; evaluated on **314 clinical questions** with significant improvement over standard LLMs across **factuality, completeness, user preference, and adversarial safety**. The inclusion of an *adversarial safety* axis, and the choice of curated rather than open-web corpora, are the two design decisions the rest of the field under-copied.
- **i-MedRAG** — Xiong et al. — **PSB 2025** (arXiv:2408.00727) — Aug 2024. Iterative medical RAG: the LLM generates follow-up queries conditioned on prior retrieval rounds. **69.68% zero-shot on MedQA**, beating prompt-engineering and fine-tuning baselines for GPT-3.5. This is the medical field's independent arrival at what the general literature calls agentic/multi-round retrieval. *Limitations:* iteration count is a tuned hyperparameter with diminishing and non-monotone returns; cost scales with rounds; no abstention branch.
- **MedGraphRAG** — Wu et al. — arXiv:2408.04187 — Aug 2024. Medically distinctive contribution is **Triple Graph Construction**: a triple-linked structure connecting *user documents → credible medical sources → controlled vocabularies*, plus U-Retrieval; evaluated on 9 medical QA, 2 health fact-checking benchmarks and one long-form set, with responses that "include credible source documentation and definitions." Read this as *provenance and vocabulary grounding*, not as a graph-retrieval advance (see `graph-structured-rag.md`).
- **BioLORD-2023** — Remy, Demuynck, Demeester — **JAMIA**, DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocae029 — 2024. Fuses UMLS clinical knowledge-graph structure with LLM-generated definitions to produce biomedical concept representations; the standard non-MedCPT choice for concept/ontology-level retrieval and clinical coding.
- **Rationale-Guided RAG** — Sohn et al. — arXiv:2411.00300 — Nov 2024. Perplexity-based filtering of retrieved passages, LLM-generated rationale queries, balanced corpus retrieval; up to **6.1%** improvement **[listing-only]**.

**2025 — the reckoning: hallucination taxonomies, evaluation audits, and safety.**

- **Medical Hallucinations in Foundation Models and Their Impact on Healthcare** — Kim et al. — arXiv:2503.05777 (v2 Nov 2025). Defines medical hallucination as output "factually incorrect, logically inconsistent, or unsupported by authoritative clinical evidence *in ways that could alter clinical decisions*." Eleven models, seven tasks. Three results reframe the whole vertical: (1) **general-purpose models were more hallucination-free than medical-specialized ones, 76.6% vs 51.3%** (MedGemma spanned 28.6–61.9%); (2) physician audits attributed **64–72% of residual errors to reasoning failures, not knowledge gaps**; (3) a clinician survey (n=70) found **91.8% had encountered medical hallucinations and 84.7% believed they could cause patient harm.** If most residual error is reasoning rather than recall, retrieval alone has a hard ceiling.
- **Bias Evaluation and Mitigation in Retrieval-Augmented Medical QA** — Ji, Zhang, Wang — arXiv:2503.15454 — Mar 2025. Systematically varies demographic attributes in queries across MedQA, MedMCQA, MMLU and EquityMedQA and measures **disparities in retrieval consistency as well as answer correctness**; majority-vote aggregation was the most effective mitigation. The finding that *retrieval itself is demographically inconsistent* is the important one — bias is not only a generator problem.
- **MedHELM** — Bedi et al. (75+ authors, Stanford) — arXiv:2505.23802 — May 2025. Clinician-validated taxonomy of **5 categories, 22 subcategories, 121 tasks developed with 29 clinicians**, plus **35 benchmarks (17 existing, 18 new)** and 9 frontier LLMs. The critical result is the *shape* of the profile on normalized accuracy: strong on Clinical Note Generation (**0.73–0.85**) and Patient Communication (**0.78–0.83**), moderate on Medical Research Assistance (**0.65–0.75**), and weakest exactly where stakes are highest — **Clinical Decision Support 0.56–0.72** and **Administration & Workflow 0.53–0.63**. Its LLM-jury reached **ICC = 0.47** with clinician ratings, *surpassing average clinician–clinician agreement of ICC = 0.43* (vs ROUGE-L 0.36, BERTScore-F1 0.44). That comparison is the honest ceiling of medical evaluation: the judges are as good as the humans, and the humans agree only moderately.
- **MIRIAD** — Zheng et al. — arXiv:2506.06091 — Jun 2025. **5,821,948** medical query–response pairs distilled from literature as a retrieval corpus; reported improving hallucination-detection F1 by **22.5–37%** **[listing-only]**.
- **A hallucination-benchmark cluster forms in 2025** and is worth knowing as a group rather than individually: **MedHallu** (Pandit et al., arXiv:2502.14302 — 10,000 QA pairs, best model F1 0.625 on "hard" hallucinations), **MedHEval** (Chang et al., arXiv:2503.02157 — 11 Med-LVLMs × 7 mitigation techniques), **MedHal** (Mehenni et al., arXiv:2504.08596 — hallucination detection with explanations), and **ClinHallu** (S. Yang et al., arXiv:2606.14697 — 7,031 instances, *stage-wise* hallucination diagnosis) **[all listing-only]**. The collective signal: detection is unsolved (best hard-case F1 ~0.63), and the 2026 move toward *stage-wise* attribution — which pipeline stage produced the hallucination — is the direction a framework should support via per-stage instrumentation.
- **Nature Medicine data-poisoning study** — Alber et al. — Nature Medicine, DOI 10.1038/s41591-024-03445-1 — 2025. Replacing **0.001% of training tokens** with medical misinformation produced harmful models that still passed benchmarks; a biomedical knowledge-graph screen captured **91.9%** of harmful content. Directly transferable to RAG: the same argument applies to poisoned *corpora*, not just poisoned pretraining.
- **A framework to assess clinical safety and hallucination rates of LLMs for medical text summarisation** — Asgari et al. — npj Digital Medicine, DOI 10.1038/s41746-025-01670-7 — 2025. An error taxonomy, an iterative experimental structure, a clinical *harm* framework, and a GUI (CREOLA); metrics derived from **18 experimental configurations and 12,999 clinician-annotated sentences**, yielding **a 1.47% hallucination rate and a 3.45% omission rate**, with prompt/workflow refinement pushing major errors below previously reported human note-taking rates. The most useful published *base rate* in the vertical, and the clearest evidence that omission, not fabrication, is the larger channel.
- **TRIPOD-LLM** — Gallifant et al. — Nature Medicine, DOI 10.1038/s41591-024-03425-5 — 2025. Reporting guideline for healthcare LLM studies: **19 main items, 50 subitems**, emphasizing transparency, human oversight, and task-specific metrics. The de facto compliance target for anything a next-gen framework wants published in a clinical journal.
- **Contradictions in Context** — Javadi et al. — arXiv:2511.06668 (v2 Apr 2026). Builds a benchmark from Australian TGA consumer-medicine-information documents, retrieves PubMed abstracts **stratified across publication years** to create controlled temporal inconsistency, and shows that **contradictions between highly similar abstracts degrade answers**: "retrieval similarity alone is insufficient for reliable medical RAG." The cleanest existing demonstration that a similarity-only retriever is a *contradiction amplifier*.
- **RAG in Medicine: A Scoping Review** — Yang et al. — arXiv:2511.05901 — Nov 2025. The field's own audit: research relies overwhelmingly on **public** data with little private-data work; retrieval is **English-centric embeddings**; generators are mostly **generic, not medical**; human evaluation focuses on accuracy/completeness/relevance/fluency with **insufficient attention to bias and safety**; applications cluster in QA, report generation, summarization, extraction. Verdict: "medical RAG remains at an early stage."
- **CARE-RAG** — Potluri et al. — arXiv:2511.15994 (GenAI for Health workshop) — Nov 2025. Using Written Exposure Therapy protocol as testbed, finds "**errors persist even when authoritative passages are provided**," and argues reasoning fidelity must be assessed as rigorously as retrieval. The retrieval→reasoning gap, localized to a clinical protocol.

**2026 — deployment, provenance, and adversarial pressure.**

- **Ask Eolas** — Waldock, Gilchrist, Ashrafian, Darzi, Dean Franklin — **npj Antimicrobials and Resistance**, DOI 10.1038/s44259-026-00187-7 — 2026. A RAG-enhanced AI clinical decision support system for antimicrobial prescribing, evaluated in a structured single-site simulation over **45 prescribing cases with 45 participants**: **zero prescribing errors vs six (Eolas App) and eight (PDF guidelines), p < 0.001**, number-needed-to-treat **1.9**. The strongest clinical-endpoint result for medical RAG published to date — and note the authors' own framing that real-world implementation still requires "live data integration, confidence calibration systems, and comprehensive auditability."
- **ACIE (Agentic Clinical Information Extraction)** — Çinar-Koraş et al. — arXiv:2606.19602 — Jun 2026. **On-premise agentic RAG deployed at University Medicine Essen.** Diagnoses why standard RAG fails on real patient records — "the document-level metadata that AI systems need for retrieval and triage is absent or incomplete," plus mishandled temporal reasoning and cross-document dependencies — and grounds every answer in source passages for clinician verification. **Across 7,326 clinician judgments, 96.5% of extractions were accepted (per-type 80–99%).** Subtitle "What Works, What Breaks, and Why" makes it the single most useful deployment paper in this file.
- **Deployment-Centered Evaluation** — Unell, Fuentes, Li, Lin, Jagadeesan, Koyejo, Shah — arXiv:2606.12702 — Jun 2026. An EHR-embedded LLM system at an academic medical center; a **pre-response classifier predicts query-level user rejection risk, AUROC 0.719 over 4.5 months**, and crucially **deployment context (provider type, department, model used) improves prediction over query content alone**. Formalizes the idea that a clinical retrieval system should know *which requests it is likely to fail* before answering.
- **DrugClaw / DrugAudit** — Q. Wang et al. — arXiv:2606.01434 — May 2026. Multi-agent RAG over a registry of drug and pharmacovigilance "skills" with a reflection-driven state machine, returning answers grounded in **primary regulatory or peer-reviewed records**. **DrugAudit** is a **3,772-item authority-aware benchmark** scoring *upstream-of-gold source match*, snippet overlap, and citation faithfulness under a dual-judge protocol (inter-judge **κ = 0.88**). DrugClaw reports **primary-source rate 0.918 (+10.1 pp over next-best)** and **faithfulness 0.887 (+5.9 pp)**. The first benchmark to treat *source authority* as a first-class scored dimension rather than a footnote.
- **GMPilot** — X. Wang et al. — arXiv:2603.20815 — Mar 2026. RAG + ReAct agent over a curated corpus of **regulations and historical FDA inspection observations** for cGMP compliance, aimed at "real-time and traceable decision support." Explicitly self-limiting on regulatory scope and interpretability; a demonstration, not a validated system.
- **MMed-Bench-IR** — J. Lee et al. — arXiv:2606.24200 — Jun 2026. Multilingual medical IR benchmark deliberately disentangling three capabilities across six languages — cross-lingual alignment, concept discrimination, evidence retrieval — with **zero concept and query overlap between tasks by design**: 6,127 UMLS-grounded cross-lingual QA queries, 4,975 confusion sets at three difficulty tiers, 2,040 quality-assured RAG evidence queries. Ten systems across six paradigm families reveal "severe cross-lingual failure": **biomedical encoders scoring 0.818 nDCG@10 in English drop to 0.056 in Japanese, "a gap that English-only benchmarks cannot detect."** The sharpest quantification of the equity failure the scoping review flagged.
- **ChronoMedKG** — M. S. Ahmed et al. — arXiv:2605.22734 — May 2026. The most directly framework-relevant temporal work in the vertical. Its premise is that existing biomedical KGs (PrimeKG, Hetionet, iKraph) "do not encode *when* a finding becomes clinically relevant," so a symptom diagnostic of one disease at age 3 may imply another at 13. Builds **460,497 evidence-linked triples** (filtered from 13M raw extractions) over **13,431 diseases**, each association tied to an **onset window or progression stage**, PMID-traceable, with a **multi-signal credibility score**, kept only under multi-model consensus plus credibility filtering plus ontology alignment. 92.7% agreement against Orphadata; adds temporal grounding for 6,250 diseases absent from HPOA/Orphadata/Phenopackets. Its **ChronoTQA** benchmark (3,341 questions, six temporal + two static control task types) shows **frontier LLMs losing roughly 30 points moving from static to temporal questions**, with ChronoMedKG retrieval rescuing **47–65% of long-tail failures against 17–29% for HPOA-RAG**. The 30-point static→temporal drop is the single best argument in this file for making temporal validity a first-class retrieval primitive.
- **MedMemoryBench** — Y. Wang et al. — arXiv:2605.11814 — May 2026. Built from the production requirements of a health-management agent serving "tens of millions of active users": ~2,000 sessions / 16,000 turns of synthetic long-horizon patient trajectories, with an **"evaluate-while-constructing" streaming protocol** and explicit study of **memory saturation** — "sustained information influx actively degrades retrieval and reasoning robustness."
- **When RAG Chatbots Expose Their Backend** — Madrid-García & Rujas — arXiv:2605.00796 — May 2026. Non-destructive audit of a *live, publicly accessible, patient-facing* medical RAG chatbot: ordinary browser DevTools inspection recovered the system prompt, model and embedding configuration, retrieval parameters, backend endpoints, API schema, chunk metadata, knowledge-base content, and **the 1,000 most recent patient–chatbot conversations, without authentication**, contradicting the product's own privacy assurances. The most concrete published RAG deployment failure in medicine and the one least represented in ML-systems discourse.
- **Knowledge Poisoning Attacks on Medical Multi-Modal RAG** — P. Yang et al. — arXiv:2605.10253 — May 2026, and **MedThreatRAG** — Zuo et al. — arXiv:2508.17215 — Aug 2025 (cross-modal conflict injection, reported F1 drops up to 27.66%) **[both listing-only]**. Query-agnostic corpus poisoning producing "clinically plausible yet incorrect generations."
- **Measuring What Matters** — Desikan et al. — arXiv:2605.08445 — May 2026. "Most existing benchmarks test what a model *knows*; too few test whether it can *perform* reliably." Frontier models near-perfect on licensing exams degrade to **0.74–0.85 on documentation, 0.61–0.76 on clinical decision support, and only 0.53–0.63 on administrative and workflow tasks** — "**High benchmark scores give a false sense of deployment readiness, and the gap between performance and utility widens precisely as AI systems take on more consequential clinical roles.**" Note the profile matches MedHELM's independently, which strengthens both.
- **Bridging the Knowledge–Action Gap (SCMPE)** — H. Ma et al. — arXiv:2601.12974 — Jan 2026. Dentistry benchmark separating static objective tasks from multi-turn simulated patient interactions. Finds a **"High Efficacy, Low Safety"** profile for general models, that the binding limitation is **active information gathering and dynamic state tracking** rather than knowledge, and that while RAG reduces hallucination in static contexts its effect in dynamic workflows is "inconsistent and sometimes counterproductive."

## State of the art — mid-2026 snapshot

1. **Medical RAG's headline benefit is real but narrow and corpus-dependent.** MIRAGE's +18% is a ceiling under favorable configuration, not an expectation: only PubMed and the aggregated MedCorp beat CoT on *all* MIRAGE tasks, retriever fusion sometimes hurts, and the gain shrinks as the backbone gets stronger. The 2026 clinical literature confirms the pattern with more granularity — knowledge-enhanced configurations beat baselines convincingly on well-specified tasks (baseline 68% → RAG 91.3% → fine-tuning+RAG 96.7% in Prabha et al., JMIR 2026, DOI 10.2196/90692), while head-to-head comparisons of a domain-specific RAG product against a strong general model can show **no statistically significant difference** (Yang SS et al., JACCP 2026, DOI 10.1002/jac5.70235).
2. **Grounding *source* dominates grounding *mechanism*.** Dukes et al. ("Guideline-anchored retrieval-augmented generation outperforms baseline and literature-only configurations in gynecologic oncology decision support: A pre-integration benchmark", Gynecologic Oncology 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.ygyno.2026.07.005) is the cleanest published ablation: 50 de-identified cases, three gynecologic oncologists, modified Generative Performance Score (guideline concordance plus hallucination penalty). **NCCN-anchored GPT-5 RAG mGPS 0.83 (SD 0.26) > literature-anchored OpenEvidence 0.70 (SD 0.27) > baseline GPT-5 0.65 (SD 0.31)**; GPT-RAG beat baseline (P < .001, r = 0.49) and OpenEvidence (P = .008), while **OpenEvidence and baseline did not differ (P = .22)** — i.e. literature-anchored retrieval bought nothing statistically over the raw model, and *normative guideline* anchoring bought a lot (OR 3.74, 95% CI 1.57–8.90). The authors also note OpenEvidence's own NCCN integration on April 27, 2026 as external validation of guideline anchoring, and explicitly caveat that "findings reflect benchmark performance, not clinical safety or improved patient outcomes." Note the inter-rater agreement: **ICC 0.49 for mGPS but only 0.30 for the Hallucination Penalty** — even expert raters barely agree on what counts as a hallucination. DrugClaw's authority-aware benchmark encodes the same source-hierarchy insight for drug information.
3. **Citation validity and clinical safety have decoupled.** Kang et al. (JMIR 2026, DOI 10.2196/93393) found a system with **fully verifiable citations (V=100%, F=0%)** that nonetheless "carried the largest Severe burden (5 of 9)" safety flags and ranked last of five. Dhaimade & Henderson (PLOS Digital Health 2026, DOI 10.1371/journal.pdig.0001072) found "citation validity was independent of answer accuracy." Meanwhile non-RAG general models fabricate citations at rates that would be disqualifying anywhere: **80% and 76% of prompts** for Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash in imaging-modality selection (Rybczyk et al., JACR 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.jacr.2026.05.012) and **26.2% fabrication** for Gemini in spine surgery (McLaughlin et al., Neurosurgery Practice 2026, DOI 10.1227/neuprac.0000000000000244). Verifiable provenance is necessary and demonstrably not sufficient.
4. **The dominant residual error mode is reasoning over retrieved evidence, not failure to retrieve it.** Kim et al. (64–72% of residual errors are reasoning failures), CARE-RAG (errors persist with authoritative passages supplied), and the SCMPE dynamic-workflow result all converge. This is the medical instantiation of the retrieval–generation gap that `graph-structured-rag.md` records for GraphRAG.
5. **"Correct but incomplete" is the characteristic clinical failure signature.** Meyer et al. (The American Surgeon 2026, DOI 10.1177/00031348261471501) name it in their title: OpenEvidence 92.8% correctness / 81.2% accuracy and ChatGPT-5 98.6% / 84.1% against NCCN rectal-cancer guidelines, both with "important limitations in completeness." Asgari et al.'s **3.45% omission vs 1.47% hallucination** puts omission at more than double fabrication. Recall-oriented retrieval metrics do not capture it.
6. **Clinicians are already using unvalidated point-of-care RAG at scale.** Barr et al. (Int J Med Inform 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2026.106589) surveyed 286 Canadian medical students: **OpenEvidence was the second-most-used LLM at 57.69%** (ChatGPT 93.36%), with only **10.95% having received formal instruction** and reported exposure to inaccurate outputs. Adoption is running well ahead of evaluation, and ahead of regulation.
7. **Domain specialization and regulatory clearance are not proxies for quality.** Kim et al.'s 76.6% vs 51.3% general-vs-medical-specialized hallucination-free gap, and Freudenberg et al. (J Med Syst 2026, DOI 10.1007/s10916-026-02434-w) finding ChatGPT-5 Thinking outperforming both OpenEvidence and a cleared specialized system (top choice in **3.6%** of rater-case combinations for OpenEvidence), both cut against the "medical model + medical corpus = safer" prior.
8. **Pharma has quietly become the most operationally mature sub-vertical, and it is *extraction*, not question answering, that works.** Ludwig et al. (Drug Safety 2026, DOI 10.1007/s40264-026-01682-6) build a two-pass OpenAI-o1 pipeline over whole EHR notes at UCSF — pass 1 screens for adverse drug events, pass 2 adds 20 structured fields — against 372 deidentified notes from 31 specialty/setting cells yielding 191 ADEs: **94.2% precision, 84.1% recall, F1 88.9%**, seriousness correct in 100% and label status in 93.9%, MedDRA lowest-level-term coding correct in 92.5%. Of valid ADEs, **12.2% met FDA "serious" criteria, 15.0% were unlabeled, 8.9% were "failure of efficacy"** — and the model surfaced ADEs the physicians never named (tacrolimus-associated hypomagnesemia). The economics are the underappreciated part: **84.9% of notes contained no ADEs at all, holding cost to USD $0.18/note and $0.35/validated ADE** — a cheap screen gating an expensive extractor is what makes the pipeline viable, and the authors explicitly target integration with **Sentinel (US) and DARWIN EU**. Complementarily, Fong et al. (Alzheimer's & Dementia: DADM 2026, DOI 10.1002/dad2.70412) analyzed **20,123 clinical documents from 46 patients** on anti-Aβ therapy at a mid-Atlantic health system, with discordant cases adjudicated to a reference label: the **LLM-augmented workflow achieved 100% sensitivity (PPV 89.7%) versus expert review's 88.6% sensitivity (PPV 100%)**. That sensitivity/PPV inversion is the key structural finding of the whole vertical in miniature — **the LLM workflow is the better *screen*, the human the better *adjudicator***, which argues for architectures that optimize recall at the retrieval/extraction stage and place precision burden on a human or verifier gate, rather than trying to make one component do both. (Both are small-n, single-site studies; the authors of the second call their own findings "preliminary indications.")
9. **Evaluation is the acknowledged bottleneck.** MedHELM's ICC = 0.47 clinician–jury agreement against 0.43 clinician–clinician agreement, Dukes et al.'s ICC 0.30 on the hallucination-penalty sub-score, Sun et al.'s judge-LLM agreement of **κ = 0.21–0.24** with human experts on medication safety (Int J Med Inform 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2026.106599), Mayer et al.'s finding that the RAG advantage in periodontology "was confounded with response length" (J Clin Periodontol 2026, DOI 10.1111/jcpe.70160), and Philipp et al.'s observation that LLM evaluators "assigned definitive scores in every case" without abstention (arXiv:2607.01103) **[listing-only]** together mean most reported medical-RAG gains have wide, mostly unreported error bars.

## Retrieval infrastructure: corpora, embeddings, and their limits

**Corpora.** MedRAG standardized four/five-corpus retrieval (PubMed, StatPearls, medical textbooks, Wikipedia, plus the aggregated MedCorp) and its finding that only PubMed/MedCorp win uniformly remains the reference point. Stepanyan & McDermott's systematic trade-off study (arXiv:2604.20853, Feb 2026) re-runs the design space across exam-style, conversational, community, and non-question queries: **corpus aggregation is best in absolute retrieval quality**, `MedRAG/pubmed` is Pareto-optimal under HNSW when restricted to one corpus, chunking strategy matters, and FAISS gives the best speed/quality balance. Bal et al. (arXiv:2605.02520) report cross-encoder reranking as the best single retrieval strategy while **multi-query expansion introduces noise** **[listing-only]** — a caution worth noting given how default query expansion has become.

**Embeddings.** Two lineages: click-log-supervised dense retrieval (**MedCPT**) and ontology-fused concept representation (**BioLORD-2023**, fusing UMLS graph structure with LLM definitions). BioLORD remains competitive for coding/normalization but the numbers are sobering in absolute terms — a 2026 FHIR/LOINC transfer study reports BioLORD achieving the **best top-rank accuracy at R@1 = 0.185** (arXiv:2606.15449) **[listing-only]**. Terminology-grounded retrieval in medicine is nowhere near solved.

**UMLS as retrieval substrate.** UMLS supplies ~3.4M concepts and ~34.2M relations (per arXiv:2604.16422) **[listing-only]** and is used three ways: to normalize queries, to constrain the retrieval graph (MedGraphRAG's controlled-vocabulary link, MedCoRAG's joint KG-path + guideline retrieval), and to *gate* answers — T2D-Bench (arXiv:2606.24145) uses UMLS knowledge graphs to verify diabetes recommendations and reports **33–35% of GPT-4o/4o-mini cases failing evidence gating** **[listing-only]**.

**The multilingual cliff.** The scoping review's "English-centric embedding models" finding is quantified by MMed-Bench-IR's **0.818 → 0.056 nDCG@10** English→Japanese drop **[listing-only]**, and partially addressed by work like Rey-Blanco et al.'s synthetic-data-trained multilingual clinical coder (Portuguese R@5 = 0.829 vs BioBERT-ST 0.714, arXiv:2605.30529) **[listing-only]**. A framework that treats the retriever as a swappable component but assumes English-quality embeddings everywhere will silently fail in most of the world.

## When RAG helps and when it hurts medical QA

This is the load-bearing evidence for anyone motivating a new framework. Ordered from most to least established:

1. **It helps most where the query is well-specified and the corpus is normative.** Guideline-anchored configurations, contraindication checking, and single-fact drug information. Tao et al. (npj Digital Medicine 2026, DOI 10.1038/s41746-026-03035-0) report a RAG-enhanced diabetic-kidney-disease system "substantially reduced safety-critical errors, particularly in identifying medication contraindications."
2. **It helps least — and can hurt — in multi-turn, state-dependent clinical interaction.** SCMPE: RAG's effect on dynamic workflows is "inconsistent and sometimes counterproductive," because the deficit is information *gathering* and state tracking, not knowledge.
3. **Contradictory retrieved evidence actively degrades answers.** Javadi et al. show similarity-ranked PubMed abstracts stratified across years produce inconsistency and reduced factual accuracy. There is no contradiction detector in a standard pipeline.
4. **Position effects are strong.** MIRAGE's U-shaped accuracy-vs-ground-truth-position curve means a *correct* retrieval placed mid-context is worth materially less than the same retrieval placed at an edge. Any framework that packs k snippets without position-aware ordering is leaving measurable accuracy on the floor.
5. **Retrieval scaling saturates.** Log-linear in k up to ~32 snippets, then flattening — an argument for spending budget on evidence *selection and structuring* rather than on more k.
6. **More retrieval can increase false alarms in safety-sensitive triage.** A mental-health RAG study reports "RAG caused a rise in false alarms" (arXiv:2607.24817) **[listing-only]** — under-discussed, because in a safety pipeline a false positive has a real cost too.
7. **Robustness overstates itself.** MamaBench reports **base accuracy overstating robust (counterfactual) accuracy by 16–28 percentage points** in maternal and child health (arXiv:2607.14385) **[listing-only]**.
8. **Retrieval improvements do not reliably translate into answer improvements.** The general-domain result — stronger retrieval increasing hallucination when truly relevant documents are absent (arXiv:2603.24580, AI policy QA) **[listing-only]** — has an exact medical analogue in CARE-RAG's persistence of errors under authoritative context.

## Provenance, authority hierarchies, and evidence grading

Medicine is the vertical where "cite your sources" is not a UX nicety but a regulatory and epistemic requirement, and where the *hierarchy* of sources is codified (systematic review > RCT > cohort > case report; label > guideline > literature > opinion).

- **DrugAudit** is the state of the art in scoring this: *upstream-of-gold source match* (did the system cite the primary regulatory record, or a downstream restatement?), snippet overlap, citation faithfulness. Primary-source rate becomes a metric, and DrugClaw's 0.918 leaves ~8% of high-stakes drug answers grounded in something other than a primary source.
- **Evidence-tier reranking** appears in EBM-adapted GraphRAG work — PICO alignment plus evidence-tier reranking over a 357,844-node graph, reporting 0.812 evidence recall@10 and 0.819 answer faithfulness (arXiv:2601.00216) **[listing-only]**.
- **Claim-level verification** is converging on a common shape: decompose the answer into atomic claims, verify each against retrieved evidence with NLI and/or KG consistency, and map the verdict to an action. MedRAGChecker (arXiv:2601.06519) does NLI + KG consistency; **Claim-Selective Certification for High-Risk Medical RAG** (arXiv:2605.21949) maps claims to **partial / full / conflict / abstain** actions; M-Eval (arXiv:2510.23995) imports **evidence-based-medicine heterogeneity analysis** to detect factual errors via multi-source disagreement, reporting ~23% accuracy improvement **[all three listing-only]**. The EBM-heterogeneity framing is the most interesting borrowing: medicine already has a formal apparatus for "these sources disagree; here is how much to trust the pooled estimate."
- **Corpus integrity is a provenance problem too.** PubMed contains retracted and fraudulent articles; Pub-Guard-LLM (arXiv:2502.15429) builds an 11K-article PubMed retraction benchmark and a retrieval-augmented detector **[listing-only]**. A medical RAG index that does not track retraction status will cite retracted trials indefinitely.

## Temporal validity: guidelines change, corpora don't

Clinical guidelines are versioned normative documents with effective dates, supersession relations, and jurisdictional scope. Almost no RAG system models any of that.

- Javadi et al. deliberately construct temporal inconsistency by stratifying retrieved PubMed abstracts by publication year and show the damage.
- **ChronoMedKG** (arXiv:2605.22734) is the strongest existing evidence that this matters: **frontier LLMs lose ~30 points moving from static to temporal clinical questions**, and its onset-window/progression-stage annotations with PMID-traceable evidence and credibility scores rescue 47–65% of long-tail failures. **Chronofy** (arXiv:2607.20560) proposes temporal-logical decay — learnable exponential decay in graph retrieval plus Signal Temporal Logic constraints on information validity **[listing-only]**; **Knowledge-driven Augmentation and Retrieval for Integrative Temporal Adaptation** (arXiv:2604.22098) folds medical ontologies into temporal-shift-aware retrieval **[listing-only]**.
- Guidelines also **conflict with each other**, especially under multimorbidity. Xie et al. (arXiv:2604.17340) automate conflict detection across multimorbidity guidelines with SAT verification, reporting **90.6% of conflicts are "Local"** and F1 = 0.861 **[listing-only]**. A retrieval layer that returns two guidelines without surfacing that they contradict for this patient is worse than one that returns neither.
- Guideline concordance measurements are contaminated by recall: Hirsch et al. (J Oral Pathol Med 2026, DOI 10.1111/jop.70140) got **perfect concordance κ = 1.0 when recommendations were presented verbatim** and explicitly note this "reflects recognition rather than autonomous clinical reasoning." Castro et al. (Braz J Otorhinolaryngol 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.bjorl.2026.101841) report **75% congruence with three responses contradicting guidelines**. Reported concordance ranges of roughly 64–96% across specialties should be read as measuring memorization plus retrieval, not judgment.

## Abstention, calibration, and deferral

Medicine is the vertical where "I don't know" has positive expected value, and the requirement is largely unmet.

- The clearest failure signal: audits repeatedly find **high confidence coexisting with error**. Ahn et al. (Neurogastroenterol Motil 2026, DOI 10.1111/nmo.70393) report OpenEvidence and Copilot at 65% diagnostic accuracy with "high confidence despite diagnostic errors ... uniform across models." A brain-MRI VLM safety audit finds **33–46% of answered items are high-confidence errors** (arXiv:2608.02790) **[listing-only]**.
- The most promising architectural response is **selective prediction with an explicit coverage knob**. Khanmohammadi et al., **"Calibrated Triage, Not Autonomy"** (arXiv:2606.15910, Jun 2026), reframes the question from accuracy to deployment — "how much imaging work a model can safely handle alone" — across seven confidence estimators, five open-weight LVLMs, three medical VQA datasets, with probes trained *only on natural images* and applied without adaptation. Four findings that a framework should internalize: (1) **standard metrics are poor guides** — discrimination barely separates methods and weak calibration of a cheap self-report is "cheaply removed by off-domain temperature scaling without changing deployable yield"; (2) what distinguishes a usable estimator is the *high-confidence region a clinician acts on* — **weakest baselines are confidently wrong on 41–45% of their errors vs 1–4% for the best probe**; (3) **no estimator is reliably best across domains or models**; (4) safe handoff is governed at two levels — base-model competence sets a ceiling (a well-calibrated score recovers "roughly a third of radiology cases at a 20 percent error tolerance but almost none of pathology"), and the confidence layer decides how much of that ceiling is reachable. Complementarily, **MANANA** (Rajesh et al., arXiv:2606.31036, Jun 2026) does learned deferral in Ugandan pediatric epilepsy care, converting observed prescription errors into **auditable prompt memories** and using Bayesian prompt averaging as an uncertainty-based deferral signal: **+4–8 pp top-3 accuracy and auto-handling the most confident half at 95% precision, or the most confident quarter at 99%**, deferring the rest. Its diagnosis that many errors reflect "distribution-miscalibrated prescribing defaults rather than failures to parse the local record" is a distinct failure mode from retrieval error: the corpus was read correctly and the prior was wrong. Coverage/risk curves, not accuracy, are the right currency.
- **Deployment-side abstention** is the Unell et al. contribution: predict rejection risk *before* generating, using deployment context, and route accordingly.
- The evaluation layer has the same gap — LLM judges that never abstain (arXiv:2607.01103) **[listing-only]** cannot certify a system whose main safety feature is abstaining.

## Clinical and pharma deployment evidence

**Hospital / clinician-facing.**
- *On-premise extraction*: ACIE at University Medicine Essen — 96.5% clinician acceptance across 7,326 judgments, every answer source-linked, with the honest diagnosis that missing document-level metadata is the actual blocker.
- *EHR-embedded assistant*: Unell et al.'s academic-medical-center system with 4.5 months of rejection data.
- *Simulation with a hard endpoint*: Ask Eolas (zero prescribing errors vs 6 and 8, NNT 1.9).
- *Clinician-evaluated curated RAG*: Almanac (314 questions; factuality, completeness, preference, adversarial safety) and Almanac Copilot for EHR navigation (**74% task completion on 300 EHR-QA queries, mean 2.45/3**, Research Square preprint 2025 — preprint, not peer-reviewed).
- *Multidisciplinary tumor board support*: VISTA Architect, EHR→persistent knowledge graph, reporting 96.4% accuracy across 15 tumor-board variables (arXiv:2606.22692) **[listing-only]**.
- *Silent-trial methodology* is appearing in protocols — e.g. local performance and fairness testing of an AI scribe in pediatric developmental assessment (McCradden et al., BMJ Open 2026, PMID 42320966), a parallel clinician-vs-AI note design. Silent/shadow deployment is the clinical field's answer to offline evaluation, and a next-gen framework should support it natively.

**Pharma / life sciences.**
- *Pharmacovigilance*: Ludwig et al. (Drug Safety 2026) — two-pass o1 over whole UCSF EHR notes, 94.2%/84.1%/88.9% P/R/F1, $0.18 per note, aimed at Sentinel/DARWIN EU integration; Fong et al. (Alzheimer's & Dementia: DADM 2026) — anti-Aβ safety monitoring across 20,123 documents from 46 patients, 100% sensitivity / 89.7% PPV vs expert 88.6% / 100%; Warner et al. (Clin Pharmacol Ther 2026, DOI 10.1002/cpt.70409) safety-signal assessment with GPT-4o case extraction, **F1 0.444–1.000 for risk factors and 0.429–0.909 for dechallenge/rechallenge responses** — the wide per-field variance is the operational reality.
- *Drug information*: DrugClaw/DrugAudit; Waikar et al. (CPT: Pharmacometrics & Systems Pharmacology 2026, DOI 10.1002/psp4.70201) report a RAG system correctly identifying indications, use in specific populations, and warnings/precautions against FDA guidance; DILIConsult, a multi-agent LLM for drug-induced liver injury (Pharmacotherapy 2026, DOI 10.1002/phar.70131).
- *Regulatory & GxP*: GMPilot for cGMP; the broader regulatory-RAG literature outside medicine (compliance-checking pipelines, citation-enforced RAG for auditability, production RAG evolution toward agentic escalation) supplies transferable patterns **[listing-only]**.
- *Clinical trial documents*: Pesántez et al. (ESMO Real World Data & Digital Oncology 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100726) evaluated a Study Document Assistant in oncology trials — **median SME score ~3/4 ("Mostly Correct") in both arms with no statistically significant accuracy difference, but a 43.8% reduction in response time.** The honest shape of most enterprise RAG value: latency and throughput, not accuracy.
- *Literature-review automation*: LLM-as-third-reviewer in orthopaedic systematic reviews with near-perfect agreement on some topics (Vivekanantha et al., KSSTA 2026, PMID 42159215); automated data extraction with **315/384 fields (82%) fully correct by both models and 365 (95.1%) by at least one** (KSSTA 2026, PMID 42015569); title/abstract screening at **91% accuracy / F1 81.6%** (Józwiak et al., Expert Rev Pharmacoecon Outcomes Res 2026, PMID 42311049) versus a chatbot full-text screening accuracy of only **0.71–0.77** (Martins et al., Int J Med Inform 2026, PMID 41950629). Fact-checking throughput is the strongest efficiency claim in the vertical: **42 seconds versus a mean 18 minutes** for expert fact-checks at 86% agreement (Ryan et al., JMIR Form Res 2026, DOI 10.2196/68223).

## Regulatory and governance constraints

FDA's own guidance web pages returned HTTP 404 to WebFetch in this pass, but I retrieved the **statutory text** from govinfo (21 U.S.C. §360j(o)(1)(E), the FD&C Act device-definition exclusion added by the 21st Century Cures Act). This is the single most important regulatory fact in this file, and it is worth quoting exactly, because it makes retrieval provenance a *statutory* requirement rather than a UX preference.

- **Device vs non-device boundary — the exact criteria.** §360j(o)(1)(E) excludes a software function from the device definition — "unless the function is intended to acquire, process, or analyze a medical image or a signal from an in vitro diagnostic device or a pattern or signal from a signal acquisition system" — where the purpose is:
  > "(i) displaying, analyzing, or printing medical information about a patient or other medical information (such as peer-reviewed clinical studies and clinical practice guidelines);
  > (ii) supporting or providing recommendations to a health care professional about prevention, diagnosis, or treatment of a disease or condition; **and**
  > (iii) enabling such health care professional to **independently review the basis for such recommendations** that such software presents so that it is not the intent that such health care professional rely primarily on any of such recommendations to make a clinical diagnosis or treatment decision regarding an individual patient."

  Read carefully, clause (iii) is a *retrieval-architecture* requirement stated in statute. To stay non-device, a RAG-based CDS tool must present the **basis** for its recommendation in a form the clinician can independently review — which means citations must be to inspectable primary sources, must actually support the specific claim, and must be complete enough that the clinician is not forced to rely primarily on the tool's conclusion. Three findings in this file bear directly on whether typical systems satisfy it: fabricated citations at 26–80% in non-RAG models; citation validity that is statistically *independent* of answer accuracy (Dhaimade & Henderson); and the "correct but incomplete" pattern (Meyer et al.), where an incomplete basis defeats independent review even when every citation resolves. Note also that clause (i)'s parenthetical explicitly names "peer-reviewed clinical studies and clinical practice guidelines" — precisely the corpora medical RAG retrieves over. *[Statutory text verified verbatim via govinfo, USCODE-2023-title21-chap9-subchapV-partA-sec360j. FDA's interpretive guidance on CDS, which I could not fetch in this pass, applies further conditions; verify against the current FDA CDS guidance before relying on the statutory reading in a paper.]*
- **EU AI Act.** Secondary literature seen in this pass states the Act "classif[ies] most AI medical tools as high-risk and subject[s] them to lifecycle monitoring" (Senguttuvan et al., J Maxillofac Oral Surg 2026, PMID 42528759), that **Article 4 mandates a "sufficient level of AI literacy"** across healthcare roles (Palmieri et al., npj Health Systems 2026, PMID 42527483), and that obligations include **AI-enabled medical device change control and lifecycle monitoring** (Tănase et al., Healthcare 2026, PMID 42450877). High-risk classification implies logging, traceability, human oversight, and data governance duties that map directly onto RAG system design.
- **Reporting.** TRIPOD-LLM (19 items / 50 subitems) is the operative reporting standard for clinical LLM studies.
- **The evidence base regulators will see is weak.** Busch et al. (Communications Medicine 2025, DOI 10.1038/s43856-024-00717-2) analyzed 89 studies across 29 specialties and found GPT-3.5 (53.2%) and GPT-4 (26.6%) dominant with hallucination, omission, non-reproducibility and bias as recurring limitations; a 2026 scoping review of DeepSeek in medicine describes the evidence as "predominantly low-quality and simulation-based, with few prospective trials" (Zhang et al., JMIR 2026, PMID 42296533).
- **Security and privacy are compliance failures, not just bugs.** The Madrid-García & Rujas audit — full system prompt, retrieval configuration, knowledge base, and 1,000 patient conversations retrievable without authentication from a live patient-facing RAG chatbot — is precisely the class of incident that ends a deployment. Confidential/federated RAG work exists (C-FedRAG, arXiv:2412.13163, which uses MIRAGE as its evaluation harness) **[listing-only]** but is not the norm.

## Failure modes & critiques

A catalogue, ordered by how much of it a next-generation framework can actually address.

1. **Similarity retrieval is a contradiction amplifier.** Top-k by embedding similarity retrieves *mutually inconsistent* evidence and the generator silently reconciles it (Javadi et al.). No standard pipeline stage detects contradiction.
2. **No temporal validity model.** Superseded guidelines and retracted trials sit in the index with the same standing as current ones. Publication date is metadata, not a first-class validity interval; supersession relations are unmodeled.
3. **Provenance without authority.** Systems cite *a* source rather than the *upstream* authoritative source; DrugAudit shows ~8% primary-source misses even in the best system. Evidence hierarchy is absent from ranking in almost all deployed systems.
4. **Verifiable citations ≠ safe answers.** Kang et al.'s V=100%/F=0% system with the worst severe-safety burden; Dhaimade & Henderson's citation-validity/accuracy independence. Provenance is being used as a proxy for safety and it does not hold.
5. **Omission dominates fabrication.** 3.45% omission vs 1.47% hallucination (Asgari et al.); "correct but incomplete" (Meyer et al.). Neither faithfulness metrics nor recall@k detect a clinically material missing contraindication.
6. **The retrieval→reasoning gap.** 64–72% of residual medical errors are reasoning failures (Kim et al.); errors persist with authoritative passages supplied (CARE-RAG). More retrieval cannot fix this.
7. **Position sensitivity.** MIRAGE's U-shaped ground-truth-position curve: correct retrieval, wrongly placed, is partially wasted.
8. **Saturating returns and hard token economics.** Log-linear to k≈32 then flat; MedMemoryBench's **memory saturation**, where sustained information influx *actively degrades* retrieval and reasoning.
9. **Static-task success does not transfer to multi-turn clinical work.** SCMPE: "High Efficacy, Low Safety"; RAG's dynamic-workflow effect inconsistent and sometimes counterproductive. The missing capability is active information gathering (asking the clarifying question), not lookup.
10. **No abstention, and confident errors.** 33–46% high-confidence errors in one imaging audit; uniform high confidence despite diagnostic error in another. Coverage/risk curves are not reported by default.
11. **Retrieval is demographically inconsistent.** Ji et al. measure disparities in *retrieval consistency* across race/gender/socioeconomic query variants — bias enters at the retriever, not only the generator.
12. **English-only competence.** nDCG@10 0.818 → 0.056 English→Japanese **[listing-only]**; the scoping review's English-centric-embeddings finding. Equity failure is a retrieval-stack failure.
13. **Corpus poisoning is cheap and effective.** 0.001% of tokens sufficed to corrupt a medical model (Alber et al.); query-agnostic multimodal RAG poisoning produces "clinically plausible yet incorrect" output **[listing-only]**. Medical corpora are partly open-submission (preprints, predatory journals) — a poisoning surface, not just a quality problem.
14. **Deployment security is routinely botched.** Full backend and 1,000 patient conversations exposed via browser DevTools on a live patient-facing system.
15. **Evaluation is not trustworthy enough to certify anything.** MedHELM ICC 0.47 (vs 0.43 clinician–clinician); expert ICC 0.30 on hallucination penalty (Dukes et al.); judge–expert κ 0.21–0.24; length confounds; benchmark saturation with 0.53–0.63 on real workflow tasks **[listing-only]**; MedCalc-Bench measuring formula memorization (~52% → 81–85% with open-book prompting, arXiv:2603.02222) **[listing-only]**; guideline concordance inflated by verbatim presentation (κ=1.0).
16. **Missing document metadata is the real-world blocker.** ACIE's central finding: the metadata retrieval and triage require is "absent or incomplete" in actual patient records. Benchmarks assume clean, dated, typed documents. Hospitals do not have them.
17. **Vendor and product claims outrun evidence.** Regulatory clearance and domain specialization did not predict quality (Freudenberg et al.); a domain RAG product tied a general model on pharmacy cases (Yang SS et al.); the RAG advantage was confounded with response length in periodontology (Mayer et al.).
18. **Adoption without training or governance.** 57.69% student use of a point-of-care RAG product with 10.95% formal instruction.

## Relevance to a next-generation agentic RAG framework

Medicine is not a special case to be handled by a plugin; it is the stress test that reveals which framework abstractions are missing. Concretely, the medical evidence above argues for the following as **core primitives**, not domain extensions:

1. **Documents carry validity intervals, not timestamps.** A first-class `validity` field (effective from / superseded by / retracted on / jurisdiction) evaluated at query time, with retrieval able to filter and rank on it, and generation able to say "the applicable guideline changed in 2025." ChronoMedKG and Chronofy are prototypes of the retrieval half; nothing standard exists for supersession.
2. **Corpora are versioned, content-addressed, and reproducible.** Given an answer and its audit record, a reviewer must be able to reconstruct the exact index state that produced it. This is a hard requirement under high-risk lifecycle monitoring and change-control obligations, and it is incompatible with an index that is mutated in place by a nightly ingest job.
3. **Source authority is a ranking signal and a scored output.** Encode an evidence hierarchy (label/monograph > guideline > systematic review > RCT > cohort > case report > preprint) and an `upstream-of-gold` notion of primary source. Report primary-source rate alongside faithfulness, as DrugAudit does. A framework that only exposes cosine similarity cannot express "prefer the label over the review article."
4. **Contradiction detection as a pipeline stage between retrieval and generation.** Pairwise NLI or KG-consistency over the retrieved set, with three outcomes: consistent → answer; resolvable by recency/authority → answer *with the resolution stated*; irreducible → surface the disagreement or abstain. Borrow EBM heterogeneity analysis rather than inventing a heuristic.
5. **Abstention as a typed return value with a coverage knob.** `{answer, partial, conflict, abstain}` (the Claim-Selective Certification vocabulary) as the generator's return type, with the operating point set by a risk–coverage curve rather than a prompt instruction. Report coverage/risk, not accuracy.
6. **Pre-answer routing on deployment context.** Unell et al. show provider type, department, and model materially improve rejection-risk prediction. A framework should let a deployment attach context features to the request and route (answer / escalate / defer to human / use a stricter pipeline) before spending generation tokens.
7. **Claim-level decomposition and per-claim grounding as the default output contract.** Not a paragraph with footnotes: a set of atomic claims each bound to a span in a specific version of a specific document, each independently verifiable, each individually abstainable. This is what makes clinician verification tractable — ACIE's 96.5% acceptance is achievable *because* every value is source-linked.
8. **Omission detection is a required metric, not an optional one.** Given a normative checklist (guideline requirements, label sections, mandatory pharmacovigilance fields), the framework should be able to evaluate *coverage against the checklist* rather than only faithfulness of what was said. "Correct but incomplete" needs a metric with a name.
9. **Audit trail as a data structure, not a log line.** Query, expansions, index version, retrieved candidates with scores, filtered/rejected candidates *with reasons*, reranker decisions, prompt, generation, per-claim verification verdicts, abstention decision, and human action. Immutable and queryable. This is the artifact a regulator, a pharmacovigilance QA reviewer, and a root-cause analysis all need, and it is also the substrate for offline replay when the corpus changes.
10. **Metadata repair is part of ingestion.** ACIE's diagnosis generalizes: enterprise corpora lack the document-level metadata retrieval needs. A framework should treat metadata inference (document type, date, authority, patient/entity linkage, supersession) as an explicit, evaluable ingestion stage with its own quality metrics — not assume it.
11. **Position-aware context assembly.** Given the U-shaped position effect, snippet ordering must be a deliberate, tunable stage. Same for budget: the log-linear-then-flat scaling says spend beyond k≈32 on structuring and verification, not on more snippets.
12. **Corpus integrity controls.** Retraction/withdrawal feeds, provenance attestation for ingested documents, poisoning detection (the biomedical-KG screen that caught 91.9% of harmful content is an existence proof), and separation of trusted from untrusted tiers with the tier exposed at answer time.
13. **Authorization- and tenancy-aware retrieval, and a secure-by-default deployment surface.** Retrieval configuration, system prompts, and chunk stores server-side only; no conversation history readable without authentication. The Madrid-García audit shows this cannot be left to the application developer.
14. **Native support for shadow/silent evaluation.** Run the new pipeline in parallel with the incumbent on live traffic, collect clinician judgments on a sample, and compare — the clinical field's standard for pre-deployment validation, and something a framework can make cheap.
15. **Multi-turn information gathering as a policy, not an accident.** SCMPE's core finding is that the deficit is in *asking*. A framework should treat clarifying-question generation and state tracking as an explicit policy with its own evaluation, distinct from the retrieval policy.
16. **Retrieval fairness evaluation built in.** Counterfactual demographic perturbation of queries with retrieval-consistency and answer-consistency reporting, plus non-English retrieval quality as a mandatory reported metric.
17. **Asymmetric screen/adjudicate staging as a first-class pipeline shape.** The clearest empirical win in the whole vertical (Fong et al.'s 100%/89.7% vs expert 88.6%/100%) comes from *not* asking one component to be both sensitive and precise. Combined with Ludwig et al.'s cost structure — a cheap first-pass screen rejecting 84.9% of inputs before an expensive structured extractor runs — this argues for a framework primitive that composes a **high-recall, low-cost retrieval/screening stage** with a **high-precision verification or human-adjudication gate**, with recall and precision targets set independently per stage and the cost/quality frontier reported for the composition rather than the parts. Most RAG frameworks today expose a single top-k retrieve→generate path with one implicit operating point.

## What medicine tells us that the general RAG literature does not

Cross-cutting synthesis, for a paper's motivation section. Four claims are much better supported in the medical literature than anywhere else, because medicine has clinician adjudication, hard endpoints, and regulatory pressure:

1. **Grounding source beats grounding mechanism, and it is measurable.** Dukes et al.'s ablation is the result the general literature lacks: literature-anchored retrieval was *statistically indistinguishable from no retrieval* (P = .22) while normative-guideline-anchored retrieval gave OR 3.74. The general RAG field argues about retrievers, rerankers, and graph topologies; medicine shows that *which corpus you are allowed to cite* dominates all of it. A framework's most valuable abstraction may be the authority-typed corpus, not the retriever interface.
2. **Provenance is necessary, insufficient, and now statutory.** §360j(o)(1)(E)(iii) requires that the clinician can independently review the basis of a recommendation. Simultaneously, medicine has demonstrated that citation validity is *independent* of answer accuracy and that a 100%-verifiable-citation system can carry the worst safety profile in its comparison class. No other vertical has both the legal requirement and the empirical disproof of the naive version of it.
3. **Retrieval is not the binding constraint; reasoning over retrieved evidence is.** 64–72% of residual medical errors are reasoning failures under physician audit (Kim et al.); errors persist when authoritative passages are supplied (CARE-RAG); a ~30-point drop moving from static to temporal questions (ChronoMedKG); performance collapsing from exam scores to 0.53–0.63 on real workflow tasks (MedHELM and Desikan et al. independently). The general literature's retrieval–generation gap is a benchmark artifact; here it is audited by physicians and attributed to a cause.
4. **Evaluation reaches its ceiling at human agreement, and human agreement is moderate.** MedHELM's LLM-jury ICC 0.47 *exceeds* clinician–clinician ICC 0.43; expert raters agree at only ICC 0.30 on whether something is a hallucination (Dukes et al.); judge–expert κ 0.21–0.24 on medication safety. This is not a "we need better judges" problem — it is evidence that for a class of high-stakes questions there is no crisp ground truth to be retrieved, which is itself an argument for abstention, disagreement-surfacing, and coverage/risk reporting rather than point accuracy.

**A caveat on the whole file.** The deployment evidence base is thin and fragile. The strongest clinical-endpoint result (Ask Eolas) is a **single-site simulation**, not live care. The best deployment paper (ACIE) is **one German academic hospital, one disease registry**. The pharmacovigilance results are **n=372 notes and n=46 patients** respectively, both single-site, with the second's authors calling them "preliminary indications." The guideline-anchoring ablation is **50 cases and three raters**. Most 2026 arXiv entries here are unreviewed preprints, several seen only as API listings. The field's own scoping review (Yang et al.) concludes medical RAG "remains at an early stage," and a 2026 review characterizes the evidence as "predominantly low-quality and simulation-based, with few prospective trials." Nothing in this file should be cited as *established* clinical efficacy; the value is in the **failure taxonomy and the requirement classes**, which are consistent across many independent weak studies, not in any single effect size.

## Open problems

1. **Contradiction and conflict resolution with correct epistemics.** Detecting disagreement is tractable; deciding *which* source wins for *this* patient — recency vs authority vs jurisdiction vs applicability of the trial population — is unsolved. Xie et al.'s SAT-based multimorbidity conflict detection is a start on the formal side; nothing connects it to retrieval ranking.
2. **Omission metrics that clinicians endorse.** No accepted way to score "the answer failed to mention the interaction that mattered." Checklist coverage is the obvious framing; no benchmark operationalizes it well.
3. **Closing the retrieval→reasoning gap.** If 64–72% of residual errors are reasoning, the retrieval community is optimizing the wrong term. What is the right division of labor between better evidence structuring, verifier models, and symbolic constraints?
4. **Calibrated abstention that survives distribution shift.** Selective-prediction thresholds transport poorly across sites (the thyroid study's "limited external threshold transportability" **[listing-only]**). Per-site recalibration protocols, and their regulatory status under change control, are undefined.
5. **Versioned-corpus evaluation.** We have no benchmark that changes its corpus under the system and measures whether answers update correctly, cite the new version, and flag the change. Javadi et al.'s year-stratified design is the closest thing and it is a diagnostic, not a benchmark.
6. **Evaluation that is trustworthy enough to certify.** ICC 0.47, κ 0.21–0.24, length confounds, abstention-free judges. Either much better human-agreement automated judges, or an accepted sampling protocol for clinician adjudication with reported cost.
7. **The specialization paradox.** Why were general-purpose models more hallucination-free than medical-specialized ones (76.6% vs 51.3%)? If medical fine-tuning trades reasoning for recall, the implication for medical RAG design — use general reasoners over curated corpora rather than medical models over broad corpora — is significant and under-tested.
8. **Non-English medical retrieval.** A 0.818→0.056 nDCG drop is not an incremental gap. Multilingual clinical embeddings, cross-lingual guideline alignment, and jurisdiction-aware corpora are all open.
9. **Robustness to corpus poisoning in open biomedical corpora.** Preprint servers and low-quality journals are ingestible by anyone. What does a defensible trust model for a biomedical index look like, and who maintains it?
10. **Long-horizon patient memory without saturation.** MedMemoryBench formalizes memory saturation; no architecture yet demonstrates graceful degradation over years of a patient's record. Consolidation, forgetting, and the interaction with legally mandated retention are unresolved.
11. **The device/non-device boundary for an agentic system.** A single-shot cited answer plausibly supports independent clinician review. A multi-step agent that queries the EHR, retrieves guidelines, and proposes a plan plausibly does not. Where the line falls, and what technical properties (provenance depth, abstention, audit trail) move a system to the safe side, is not settled — and this is the highest-leverage unanswered question for anyone building agentic RAG for clinical use.
12. **Cost of rigor.** Claim decomposition, per-claim verification, contradiction checking, and dual-judge evaluation multiply inference cost. Ludwig et al.'s $0.18/note is the only clean cost datum in this file and it comes from an *extraction* pipeline whose cheap screen rejects 84.9% of inputs. Nobody has published the accuracy-per-dollar frontier for safety-instrumented medical *generation*, and without it the safe configuration will keep losing procurement decisions to the fast one.
13. **What is the right operating point, and who sets it?** Selective prediction gives a coverage knob, but nothing in the literature says who chooses the threshold, on what basis, or how it is governed. A 20% error tolerance recovers a third of radiology and almost no pathology (Khanmohammadi et al.) — so the threshold is simultaneously a clinical-risk decision, a business decision, and, under high-risk lifecycle monitoring, a change-controlled parameter. There is no accepted process for setting or re-approving it, and no framework exposes it as a governed artifact.
14. **Ground truth that does not exist.** ICC 0.30 among expert raters on whether an output is a hallucination is not measurement noise to be engineered away; it says a meaningful fraction of clinical questions have no single defensible answer. What should a retrieval system *return* in that regime — the distribution of expert positions, the strongest evidence on each side, an abstention? No benchmark rewards any of these, and every current metric penalizes all three relative to confidently picking one side.
